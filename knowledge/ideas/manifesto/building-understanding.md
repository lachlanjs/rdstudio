Equipped with more sufficient maps, we consider processes for using them to further and deepen our understanding of the landscape they represent.

Maps are tools which serve certain purposes:

- Getting from one location to another efficiently
- Understanding where to situate new things that we add to the landscape
- Understanding where things are to be careful of
- Which places are out of reach

By exercising the map in service of certain purposes/goals, we can generate a teleological understanding of the map which makes it the most useful to us.

It is one thing to know all of the nodes and all of the connections in the map, but another way to know it is to use it and to experience it in various ways. The following interventions offer ways to do this, mostly qualitatively with some notable quantitative avenues as well. There is no wrong or right way to employ these techniques and this list is not exhaustive.

## Proposed Intervention: Journeying upon the Map

Perhaps the chief purpose of a map is to tour it. One could pick a natural starting point and traverse the map in some way, taking note of what comes to mind during the process. One can also recall where they went, as a way of strengthening their understanding of the relationships. This reinforces a behaviour which becomes useful in application to real problems, as those retrospective pathways are more natural and require less cognitive effort. 

## Proposed Intervention: Pathfinding

If touring is the chief purpose, then finding a route between A and B is its attendant. One can repeat the aforementioned exercise, but rather than beginning in a random location, they can pick two distant important topics and find a sensible route between them, of which there may be many and the path/connection may not be obvious. This induces a mindset less of adventure and more of questing, in which different human motivations arise and apply. A reflection on the eventual path found takes on a new teleology.

## Proposed Intervention: Reciting Landmarks

A form of learning with a storied and staunchly defended place in the history of education is rote learning. While in theory, one can be able to recite a great deal of factual information off by heart without actually understanding it, it continues to be demonstrated that rote learning is an effective educational technique.

The likely reason for this is that when the quantity of information reaches a threshold of magnitude, the learner must develop clever ways of compressing the knowledge, which implicitly develops relational understanding. 

Another reason for the success of this learning technique and its delivery in the education system is that it is very easy to assess. This is to our advantage.

## Proposed Intervention: Filling in gaps

On some maps one might pictorially represent a gray area which is unknown to the cartographer, perhaps using clouds. Before developing an understanding of a landscape, a great deal of a map should be a gray area to the individual. One can force this to be the case by removing certain nodes and asking where it went. A person who is able to know the important landmarks off by heart is of course able to solve this problem as a corollary. Like the relationship between exploring and questing elicited earlier, the exercise of filling in gaps provides teleological purpose to the act of knowing nodes "off-by-heart".

Interestingly, this is also a key technique for pretraining foundation models on textual data (missing word prediction).

## Proposed Intervention: Solving Problems

This is the most valuable and complex way to test the understanding that we build. There is more to say however in light of the other interventions. The degree to which the problem solving process and outcome results in an *increase* in our understanding, rather than a mere *application* of it, is dependent on our level of effort. 

Echoing the techniques above, we can:

- Recall in detail the articles of knowledge that we employed (recital)
- Check whether we missed any important ideas, especially if we were unsuccessful (filling)
- Examine the path through our understanding we took to solve a problem (journeying)
- Consider whether there are other ways to join the preconditions of our problem to the outcome (pathfinding)

These techniques enhance the ability of the problem to have an impact on our future understanding. By employing these techniques on our knowledge base alone before attempting to solve problems, we may be better equipped to do so in a problem solving context when it arises.

## In summary

Building a comprehensive understanding of a complex system should be done through multiple means providing several perspectives from which to understand the map's relationship to the landscape.

As noted, some of these techniques yield to quantitative judgement. More precise ways of examining our understanding are the subject of the following sections.
